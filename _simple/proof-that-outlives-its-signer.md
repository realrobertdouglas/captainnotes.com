---
title: "Proof That Outlives Its Signer, for a five-year-old"
---

If you sign a note, people can tell it is yours because they know your handwriting. But after a hundred years, nobody is left who remembers your handwriting. Your note is still a note. It is just not proof anymore.

Long ago, towns fixed this with a big book. A notary wrote every deal in the book, and the book stayed in town after the notary died. If you lost your paper, the book could give you a new one. People trusted the book, not the handwriting.

Computer signatures are like locks. Old locks get easier to pick as the years go by. So the trick is the same as the big book: a trusted bookkeeper writes down "I saw this signature today," and keeps writing it into newer, stronger books. Then you do not have to trust the old lock. You only have to trust the date.

Bandwidth Swap has bookkeepers called Mints that write down every receipt they settle. That is the big book. But the paper does not say how long a receipt is good for, or how to switch to a stronger lock when the old one gets weak, or what happens to the book if a Mint disappears.

The answers are old ones. Say how long a receipt lasts. Let links pick a stronger lock. And have the Mints keep copies of each other's books, the way towns kept the notaries' books.
