Communication lets independent actors learn whether they can work together. When people can send messages, keep ledgers, and check what actually arrived, they can judge who delivers and who does not. That judgment is what makes commerce practical. Once commerce is practical, violence loses its appeal. Attacking a trading partner also ends what the partner was providing.

Reputation forms wherever parties can communicate more than once and remember the results. A trader who delivers the ore they promised is sought out again. One who breaks the bargain is avoided. The information does not have to be perfect or immediate. It only has to be good enough that the value of future deals outweighs the short-term gain from taking something by force. Violence remains the final authority, the way to impose an outcome when nothing else works. It stops being the ordinary method once reliable exchange is possible. Communication is what makes the alternative visible.

Distance changes how communication works without removing the need for it. Across light-years, a message may take years to arrive. Continuous command is impossible. What can still travel is technical knowledge, standard agreements, records of performance, and scientific results. A group that has learned to draw power from its star, or to turn regolith into steel, can transmit the method in a single broadcast. Others with local energy and fabrication can apply it without further direction from the source. Communication here acts as a shared store of working knowledge rather than a channel for orders. Projecting force across the same distances stays expensive. Moving usable information is cheap, and it gets cheaper with better storage and error correction.

The same pattern appears on smaller scales. Early groups form where natural energy and diverse living systems are already concentrated: plants, animals, soils, and the minerals under them. Communication inside and between those groups allows specialization. One village learns to work its local flint; another learns to keep grain through the winter; both gain by trading. When the easy local options run out, further growth requires either new energy or the ability to turn available matter into whatever is required. Knowledge of how to do that travels by the same routes that once carried simpler techniques. Groups that can receive and use the knowledge depend less on any single natural deposit. Groups that cannot use it stay tied to whatever scarce deposits remain. Those are exactly the resources rulers have always found easiest to control.

Communication and commerce therefore undercut the scarcity rulers rely on. While converting matter is costly and the methods are tightly held, control of the original resource still gives leverage; whoever holds the only uranium mine sets the price. Once the techniques spread and local energy is sufficient to use them, a particular deposit loses its unique value. What stays scarce is organized capacity: working equipment and the habits that keep it from being destroyed or seized by force. Those habits come from repeated communication: shared ways to measure results, ways to settle disputes short of violence, and records that tell reliable partners from unreliable ones. None of that can be dug out of a hillside.

Communication does not have to be free or everyone honest. It only has to make, for enough people, the returns from continued exchange clearer than the returns from raiding. When that holds, systems without permanent rulers can last. They last not because conflict disappears, but because the information needed to prefer trade is available and the cost of imposing rule from far away is not. A twenty-year message cannot carry an order worth obeying, but it can carry a blueprint. Whether on a planet or between stars, authority runs up against two limits. Force is hard to project, and communication makes coordination easier than command.
