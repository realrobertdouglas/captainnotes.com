Energy is the capacity to rearrange the world. Rulers have always drawn their leverage from scarce resources fixed to a place: the fertile valley, the mine, the fishery, the oil field. Whoever holds the deposit can tax whoever needs it. This note follows what happens to that leverage once a civilization has enough energy to make the deposit unnecessary.

## Where development starts

Some arrangements of matter already hold energy in usable concentrations: coal beds, oil reservoirs, uranium deposits, falling water, sunlight captured by plants. They are not evenly spread. Britain's coal lay next to its iron; most places get one or neither. A people that starts with such concentrations, and the materials to use them, develops faster than one that does not.

The early surplus is biological as much as technical. A river valley hands over fish, timber and clay at almost no energy cost. A landscape with both the energy source and the living systems that turn it into food, fiber and tools gives a population runway. It can grow, specialize and accumulate knowledge before it has to invent costly substitutes. For a while, nature does the work.

## When the easy sources run out

That runway is finite. Soils exhaust, as Mesopotamia's did under irrigation. Accessible deposits run out. Local diversity thins, and the climate can shift past what the existing ecology tolerates. Once the low-cost options are gone, growth needs either new energy sources or the ability to turn the matter at hand into the matter that is needed.

Sufficient energy opens the second path. Fission, fusion, or any process that releases a large fraction of a fuel's rest mass can power the transformations in principle. Common elements become rare ones; bulk rock becomes structured material. Once the energy budget is large enough and the conversions reliable, the line between "resource" and "waste" disappears. Asteroids, comets and planetary crust become feedstock. A star system stops being a set of deposits. It becomes a reservoir of atoms that can be reordered.

## Development detaches from the map

Primitive development clusters where natural concentrations already exist and the energy to use them is modest. Advanced development does not. A civilization that can move from star to star, or from asteroid to asteroid within a system, no longer needs the original flora, fauna or ore bodies. It needs reactors, fabricators, and the energy to run them.

A reactor and a fabricator parked on a carbonaceous asteroid need nothing from home but the plans. They turn what they find into the tools for the next step. Exploration stops being a search for abundance that already exists. It becomes the routine collection of mass and energy wherever they are cheapest to reach.

## What this does to rulers

Once matter can be converted at acceptable cost, and the energy comes from sources as common as starlight or fusion fuel, the specific deposit loses its unique value. A distant authority that once taxed the mine finds that the mine can be replicated, or bypassed, by anyone with enough energy and the knowledge to apply it.

What stays scarce is the organized capacity to rearrange atoms: engineering knowledge, capital equipment, and institutions that keep both from being destroyed or seized. The atoms are everywhere; the know-how is not.

Distance makes the same point from the other side. As [the earlier note on distance](/future/2026/10/17/anarchy-across-the-stars.html) argued, projecting rule across light-years costs more than it returns. When local conversion replaces dependence on any one deposit, the returns shrink further and the sums get worse.

Rulers keep an edge only where a natural deposit is still the cheapest source of energy or materials, or where they can stop others from learning the techniques. Everywhere else the binding constraint shifts from owning the resource to mastering the processes that make it unnecessary.
