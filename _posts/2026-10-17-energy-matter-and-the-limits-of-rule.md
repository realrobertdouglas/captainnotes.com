Energy is the capacity to rearrange the world. Some arrangements of matter already contain it in usable concentrations: coal beds, oil reservoirs, uranium deposits, falling water, sunlight captured by plants. These are not evenly distributed. Britain’s coal lay next to its iron; most places get one or neither. A species that starts with access to such concentrations, and to the materials needed to extract and use them, develops faster than one that does not. The early surplus is biological as much as technical.

Diverse flora and fauna, together with the soils, waters, and minerals that support them, supply calories, materials, and the work living systems do for free. A river valley hands over fish, timber, and clay at almost no energy cost. A landscape that holds both the energy source and the living systems able to turn it into food, fiber, and tools gives a population runway. It can grow, specialize, and accumulate knowledge before it has to invent costly substitutes. For a while, nature does the work.

That runway is finite. Soils exhaust, as Mesopotamia’s did under irrigation; accessible deposits run out; local diversity thins; the climate can shift beyond what the existing ecology tolerates. When the low-cost options are gone, further development requires either new energy sources or the ability to convert whatever matter is on hand into the matter that is needed. Sufficient energy makes the second path available. Fission, fusion, or any process that releases a large fraction of a fuel’s rest mass can, in principle, power the transformations that turn common elements into rarer ones and bulk rock into structured materials. Once the energy budget is large enough and the conversions are reliable, the distinction between “resource” and “waste” collapses. Asteroids, comets, and planetary crust become feedstock. A star system stops being a set of deposits. It becomes a reservoir of atoms that can be reordered.

Primitive development therefore clusters where natural concentrations already exist and where the energy required to use them is modest. Advanced development detaches from those concentrations. A civilization that can move from star to star, or even from asteroid to asteroid within a system, does not need the original flora, fauna, or ore bodies. It needs reactors, fabricators, and the energy to run them. A reactor and a fabricator parked on a carbonaceous asteroid need nothing from home but the plans. They turn what they find into the tools for the next step. Exploration stops being a search for abundance that already exists. It becomes the routine collection of mass and energy wherever they are cheapest to reach.

Under these conditions the classic advantage of rulers diminishes. Their leverage has always been control over scarce resources fixed to a place: the fertile valley, the mine, the fishery, the oil field. Those who hold the resource can extract tribute from those who need it. Once matter can be converted at acceptable cost, and once the energy to do so can be obtained from ubiquitous sources such as stellar output or fusion fuel, the specific deposit loses its unique value. A distant authority that once taxed the mine now finds that the mine can be replicated, or bypassed, by anyone with sufficient energy and the knowledge to apply it. What stays scarce is not the atoms but the organized capacity to rearrange them. That means engineering knowledge, capital equipment, and institutions that keep both from being destroyed or seized by force. The atoms are everywhere; the know-how is not.

Distance reinforces the shift. As [the earlier essay on distance](/future/2026/10/17/anarchy-across-the-stars.html) argued, projecting rule across light-years is expensive relative to the returns. When local conversion replaces dependence on any one deposit, the returns shrink further and the sums get worse. What persists is the ability of local groups to generate energy, rework the matter around them, and trade the products with others who can do the same. Rulers keep an edge only where a natural deposit is still the cheapest source of energy or materials, or where they can still stop others from learning the techniques. Everywhere else, the binding constraint shifts from owning the resource to mastering the processes that make it unnecessary.
