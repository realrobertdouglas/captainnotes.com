Anarchism holds that government is both unnecessary and harmful, and that people can live justly and cooperatively without it. The word is Greek, *anarchos*: without rulers, without authority.

Suppose humanity one day meets others. A galactic council forms. Delegates arrive, treaties are drafted, and a seat is offered. For a time the council might hold. Data is shared, non-aggression pacts are signed, and a comet on a bad trajectory gets a coordinated response. Then the distances assert themselves.

The geometry of spacetime undercuts durable central rule. Information cannot travel faster than light. A message to a colony twenty light-years distant takes twenty years to arrive and twenty more for the reply. Orders issued from a capital are already decades out of date when they reach the edge. Fleets sent to enforce compliance cost more than any tax, tribute, or loyalty they can extract from the target. At sufficient distance the cost of governing exceeds the value of governing. What remains is local order or local disorder.

This is not merely a technical inconvenience. It is a structural limit. Rome could hold Britain because a legion could march there in a season; no fleet crosses twenty light-years in a lifetime. Any polity large enough to encompass systems separated by years of light-time will either fragment into self-governing units or expend itself trying to prevent that fragmentation. The second option is expensive and brittle; the first is the default once the travel time exceeds the half-life of political consensus. Anarchism, in this setting, is less an ideology than an engineering observation. Beyond a certain radius rulers cannot reliably project authority, so the systems that survive are the ones that never needed them.

The practical question is therefore how to build those local systems so that the absence of rulers does not default to predation. Violence remains the final authority. Every other kind of authority rests on it: custom, contract, currency, and reputation all depend either on a credible capacity for force or on the shared expectation that force will not be used. Where that expectation collapses, the remaining options are submission or fight. The task is to keep the expectation intact for as long as possible.

Reliable trade is one of the more effective ways to do so. When two parties know they can exchange goods, data, or specialized labor on repeated occasions, each has a standing reason not to destroy the other. A raid may yield a one-time haul, a hold full of refined ore; it also ends the stream of future exchanges and tells every other potential partner that the raider is a bad counterparty. In environments where populations are sparse, specialized skills are scarce, and capital is costly to move, that lost stream is often more valuable than the plunder. The incentive calculation tilts toward continued commerce rather than conquest. Over time the parties that learn this become the ones others prefer to deal with. They learn it through reputation systems, escrow, mutual insurance, or simply the habit of keeping accounts. Those that do not are left isolated, which is its own cost.

None of this eliminates the capacity for violence. It changes the conditions under which violence is the rational choice. Clear, transferable claims to resources, a registry that says who holds which asteroid, reduce the occasions on which parties must fight to establish who owns what. Predictable ways of settling disputes lower the chance that disagreements escalate to force: arbitration that both sides have reason to accept, or simple exit when they do not. Open entry into trade and production makes predation less attractive relative to production. These are not guarantees. They raise the price of violence relative to everything else. Where they are absent, distance alone does not produce peace. It produces isolated strongmen or mutual raiding until someone invents the missing pieces.

The same logic applies whether the “aliens” are literal or merely the inhabitants of the next valley, the next continent, or the next orbital habitat. Scale changes the numbers; it does not change the underlying constraint. Authority that cannot be cheaply enforced will not be stably exercised. The remaining choice is between order that emerges from repeated voluntary exchange and order imposed by whoever happens to hold the local monopoly on force at any moment. The former is more pleasant for everyone who is not that monopolist. It is also more stable, because it never has to send fleets across distances that would bankrupt the sender.

Anarchism, read this way, is not the claim that conflict disappears. It is the claim that the institutions capable of containing conflict without permanent rulers are both possible and, beyond a certain scale, necessary. The galactic council may still exist for the questions that actually require coordination across light-years. Everything else will be settled closer to home. The people there have more to gain from trading with their neighbors than from ruling them.
