Anarchism holds that government is both unnecessary and harmful, and that people can live justly and cooperatively without it. The word is Greek, *anarchos*: without rulers. The usual argument about it is moral. This note makes a narrower claim. Across interstellar distances anarchism is less an ideology than an engineering observation. Nobody can be ruled from twenty light-years away, so the question at the edge is never whether people govern themselves, but how.

## Why the council fails

Suppose humanity one day meets others. A galactic council forms. Delegates arrive, treaties are drafted, and a seat is offered. For a time the council holds. Data is shared, non-aggression pacts are signed, and a comet on a bad trajectory gets a coordinated response.

Then the distances assert themselves. A message to a colony twenty light-years out takes twenty years to arrive and twenty more for the reply. An order from the capital is decades stale when it lands. A fleet sent to enforce it costs more than any tax, tribute or loyalty it could collect. At sufficient distance the cost of governing exceeds the value of governing.

Rome could hold Britain because a legion could march there in a season. No fleet crosses twenty light-years in a lifetime. Any polity that spans years of light-time will either break into self-governing pieces or exhaust itself trying not to. The second is expensive and brittle. The first happens on its own once a reply takes longer than a government lasts.

So the council keeps the few questions that truly need an answer across light-years. Everything else is settled closer to home, with no ruler above. The practical question is how to build those local arrangements so that the absence of rulers does not default to predation.

## Violence stays; trade changes the arithmetic

Violence remains the final authority. Custom, contract, currency and reputation all rest on it in the end, either on someone's credible ability to use force or on everyone's expectation that it will not be used. Where that expectation collapses, the choices are submit or fight. The task is to keep the expectation intact for as long as possible.

Reliable trade is the strongest tool for that. Two parties who exchange ore, data or skilled labor again and again each have a standing reason not to destroy the other.

A raid yields a hold full of refined ore, once. It also ends every future exchange and tells every other potential partner that the raider is a bad counterparty. Where populations are sparse, skills are scarce and capital is costly to move, that lost stream is usually worth more than the plunder. The arithmetic tilts toward commerce.

Over time the parties that learn this become the ones everyone prefers to deal with. They learn it through reputation, escrow, mutual insurance, or simply the habit of keeping accounts. The ones that never learn it are left alone, which is its own cost.

## What has to be in place

Trade raises the price of violence relative to everything else. It does not make violence impossible. Three arrangements decide which way the arithmetic falls.

- **Clear, transferable claims.** A registry that says who holds which asteroid means nobody has to fight to find out.
- **A predictable way to settle disputes.** Arbitration both sides have reason to accept, or simply the option to walk away.
- **Open entry.** When anyone can join in trade and production, predation looks worse by comparison.

None of these are guarantees. Where they are missing, distance alone does not produce peace. It produces isolated strongmen, or mutual raiding until someone invents the missing pieces.

## The same rule at every scale

Nothing here depends on the aliens being literal. The next valley, the next continent, the next orbital habitat: scale changes the numbers, not the constraint. Authority that cannot be enforced cheaply will not be exercised stably.

That leaves two kinds of order. One grows out of repeated voluntary exchange. The other is imposed by whoever holds the local monopoly on force this year. The first is more pleasant for everyone who is not that monopolist. It is also more stable, because it never has to send fleets across distances that would bankrupt the sender.

Anarchism, read this way, is not the claim that conflict disappears. It is the claim that institutions able to contain conflict without permanent rulers are possible, and beyond a certain radius necessary. The galactic council can keep its seat for the questions that need it. The people at the edge have more to gain from trading with their neighbors than from ruling them.
